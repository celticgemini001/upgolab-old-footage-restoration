Restoring Old Family Video Without a Workstation

Old footage fails in four independent layers — interlacing, noise, resolution, and codec damage — and each one needs a different tool. The order matters less than people claim. The scale factor matters more.

14 November 2026 · ffmpeg order test + resolution measurements

A box of DV tapes or a phone video from 2011 has four problems at once, and they are not the same problem. Two of them are famously confused for each other, which is why so much restoration advice is either redundant or actively harmful.

The four layers, kept separate

  1. Interlacing — two fields per frame, captured at different times. Shows as comb teeth on motion. Must be deinterlaced once, and only once.
  2. Noise — sensor and tape noise, uncorrelated between frames and between neighbouring pixels. Genuinely removable, at a cost.
  3. Resolution — actual pixel count. Not recoverable, only inferable.
  4. Codec damage — block edges and ringing baked in at capture or transcoding. Not removable by any later step.

Layers 3 and 4 are the ones people try to fix with sharpening. That never works: sharpening raises the contrast of everything, including the block edges from layer 4, so damaged footage comes back with more visible damage. The right move for layer 4 is to accept it, and the right move for layer 3 is to change the target rather than the filter.

Does the deinterlace/denoise order matter? Measured: barely.

I built an interlaced test frame by combining the even rows of one image with the odd rows of a 2px-shifted copy — the same comb structure a real interlaced capture produces. Then I ran both orders with the same filters:

OrderComb residualHF energy
deinterlace → denoise5.8250.7680
denoise → deinterlace5.8700.7684

Comb residual differs by 0.8%, high-frequency energy by 0.05%. If you were expecting a large effect, this is the useful negative result: with a spatial-only denoiser, the order is nearly irrelevant. Both orders leave comb residue around 5.8, which is high — the filters here are gentle, and real interlaced footage usually needs a stronger deinterlacer than yadif's default, not a different order.

Where order does matter. Temporal denoisers, which average across frames, behave badly on interlaced input because adjacent frames are not adjacent fields. Deinterlace first when using a temporal denoiser. With spatial denoisers, skip the worry.

What actually determines the outcome: scale factor

From separate measurements on the same pipeline: Lanczos upscaling retains 21.5% of the source's high-frequency energy at 2× and 1.3% at 4×. A 480p home video to 1080p is 2.25× — in between, and acceptable. The same 480p to 4K is 4.5×, and the detail information retained collapses.

So the single most effective restoration decision is not the filter chain. It is how far you are trying to stretch the source. Going to 2× the source and stopping will look better than going to 4× with a fancier pipeline, every time.

A pipeline that respects the layers

  1. Deinterlace once, correctly. If the source is interlaced (check a motion frame at 100%), this is first and non-negotiable. Do not deinterlace progressive content — it costs real resolution.
  2. Denoise gently. Gentle matters: an aggressive denoiser removes the film grain that makes footage look like film, and the result reads as plastic. Two light passes beat one heavy pass.
  3. Upscale at 2×, not 4×. Then, if you need more, do a second 2× pass — measured separately, two 2× steps retain more than one 4× step in most pipelines because each step's model operates in its trained range.
  4. Do not sharpen the compression artifacts. If block edges are visible, sharpening makes them worse. Fix the block edges first (a dedicated deblock), or accept them.
  5. Grade last. Any colour or contrast adjustment is a per-pixel multiplication and will amplify whatever noise survived. Doing it after denoise means you amplify less.

The honest limit

Restoration recovers structure, not truth. A face at 480p contains enough information to infer that a face is there and roughly what it looked like. It does not contain the eyelashes. A model that draws convincing eyelashes has invented them, and on a family photograph that is a strange thing to do without noticing.

The test that matters: does the restored version look like the person you remember, or like a plausible stranger? If you cannot tell, compare against a still from the same era at higher resolution. The model's idea of a face and your grandmother's actual face are different things, and only one of them is in the file.

# both orders, same filters
