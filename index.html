Restoring Old Family Video Without a Workstation

Old footage fails in four independent layers — interlacing, noise, resolution, and codec damage — and each one needs a different tool. Order barely matters for deinterlace and matters a lot for denoise. The scale factor matters most.

14 November 2026 · ffmpeg order, denoise-strength and blocking measurements

A box of DV tapes or a phone video from 2011 has four problems at once, and they are not the same problem. Two of them are famously confused for each other, which is why so much restoration advice is either redundant or actively harmful.

The four layers, kept separate

  1. Interlacing — two fields per frame, captured at different times. Shows as comb teeth on motion. Must be deinterlaced once, and only once.
  2. Noise — sensor and tape noise, uncorrelated between frames and between neighbouring pixels. Genuinely removable, at a cost.
  3. Resolution — actual pixel count. Not recoverable, only inferable.
  4. Codec damage — block edges and ringing baked in at capture or transcoding. Not removable by any later step.

Layers 3 and 4 are the ones people try to fix with sharpening. That never works: sharpening raises the contrast of everything, including the block edges from layer 4, so damaged footage comes back with more visible damage. The right move for layer 4 is to accept it, and the right move for layer 3 is to change the target rather than the filter.

Does the deinterlace/denoise order matter? Measured: barely.

I built an interlaced test frame by combining the even rows of one image with the odd rows of a 2px-shifted copy — the same comb structure a real interlaced capture produces. Then I ran both orders with the same filters:

OrderComb residualHF energy
deinterlace → denoise5.8250.7680
denoise → deinterlace5.8700.7684

Comb residual differs by 0.8%, high-frequency energy by 0.05%. If you were expecting a large effect, this is the useful negative result: with a spatial-only denoiser, the order is nearly irrelevant. Both orders leave comb residue around 5.8, which is high — the filters here are gentle, and real interlaced footage usually needs a stronger deinterlacer than yadif's default, not a different order.

Where order does matter. Temporal denoisers, which average across frames, behave badly on interlaced input because adjacent frames are not adjacent fields. Deinterlace first when using a temporal denoiser. With spatial denoisers, skip the worry.

Denoise strength: hqdn3d stops working long before it stops trying

The order test above used gentle settings, so I went back and asked a different question: how hard do you have to push the denoiser before noise actually goes away, and what does that cost in detail? Same 480p frame, gaussian noise at sigma 10 added, upscaled 2× to 960×1440 with lanczos. Reference is the clean frame at the same resolution.

Read the hqdn3d rows and the collapse is obvious: it peaks at spatial 32, removes 20.4% of the noise, and then gets worse in every direction. At spatial 96 it removes less noise (10.2%) than at 20. This is not a tuning failure — hqdn3d is a bilateral-style spatial smoother, and once its range window is wide enough to smooth out sigma-10 noise it also smooths across edges. The filter is averaging real detail into the flat areas it creates. PSNR peaks at the same place, which is why tuning by PSNR alone lands you on the least-bad hqdn3d setting rather than a good one.

nlmeans changes the arithmetic. At s=3 it removes 64.8% of the noise while keeping 106.8% of the HF energy — more than the clean reference, because the noise it leaves behind is itself high-frequency. So "denoising destroys detail" needs qualification: spatial denoisers do, patch-based ones mostly don't. Non-local means finds similar patches elsewhere in the frame and averages those, which is how it can hit sigma-10 noise without flattening texture.

Where the denoise goes in the chain does matter. Measured both ways with nlmeans s=6: denoise before the 2× upscale leaves residual σ 1.82 at HF 87.4%; denoise after the upscale leaves σ 3.04 at HF 89.8%. Denoising first is 40% lower residual noise for 2.4 points of HF — the one order effect in this article large enough to plan around, and the opposite of the gentle deinterlace/denoise result above.

Practical version: hqdn3d is a deblocker with a denoise label, useful at spatial 4–8 for light cleanup and actively counterproductive past 32. If the noise is real and you want it gone, use nlmeans at s=3–6 before the upscale. Raising the hqdn3d number and hoping it will start behaving like a denoiser is the one thing this table rules out.

What actually determines the outcome: scale factor

From separate measurements on the same pipeline: Lanczos upscaling retains 21.5% of the source's high-frequency energy at 2× and 1.3% at 4×. A 480p home video to 1080p is 2.25× — in between, and acceptable. The same 480p to 4K is 4.5×, and the detail information retained collapses.

So the single most effective restoration decision is not the filter chain. It is how far you are trying to stretch the source. Going to 2× the source and stopping will look better than going to 4× with a fancier pipeline, every time.

Does sharpening amplify the block edges? Measured: yes, by 12.7%

The list above claims sharpening makes codec damage worse. That is easy to assert and easy to test, so I encoded the clean 480p frame at JPEG q18 to create real 8×8 block edges, decoded it, and put it through unsharp at three amounts, both before and after the 2× upscale.

Blockiness here is the mean absolute pixel difference across 8-pixel grid boundaries divided by the mean difference across non-boundary neighbours. A frame with no grid structure scores close to 1.0; higher means visible blocking.

Sharpening at any amount raises blockiness, monotonically, and unsharp 2.0 after the upscale takes it from 1.454 to 1.639 — a 12.7% increase in grid structure on footage that already had too much. The claim holds, and now it has a number.

The unexpected half is the split between the two columns. Sharpening before the upscale adds less blockiness (1.574 vs 1.639) but costs far more PSNR (25.25 vs 28.10) — nearly 3 dB. The upscale that follows a pre-sharpen pass smears the amplification into soft halos and loses real detail, which the PSNR catches and the blockiness metric does not. Sharpening after the upscale keeps PSNR higher but writes the grid back in at full strength on a frame where the grid is already 16 pixels wide.

Neither ordering is a fix, which is the point. The only correct move for layer 4 is a dedicated deblock before anything else — and if you do not have one, do not sharpen. A soft frame with hidden blocking reads better than a crisp frame with visible blocking, because the eye finds regular structure far faster than it finds blur.

A pipeline that respects the layers

  1. Deinterlace once, correctly. If the source is interlaced (check a motion frame at 100%), this is first and non-negotiable. Do not deinterlace progressive content — it costs real resolution.
  2. Denoise gently. Gentle matters: an aggressive denoiser removes the film grain that makes footage look like film, and the result reads as plastic. Two light passes beat one heavy pass.
  3. Upscale at 2×, not 4×. Then, if you need more, do a second 2× pass — measured separately, two 2× steps retain more than one 4× step in most pipelines because each step's model operates in its trained range.
  4. Do not sharpen the compression artifacts. If block edges are visible, sharpening makes them worse. Fix the block edges first (a dedicated deblock), or accept them.
  5. Grade last. Any colour or contrast adjustment is a per-pixel multiplication and will amplify whatever noise survived. Doing it after denoise means you amplify less.

How much of your file is duplicate frames? Measured: 60%

Before choosing a model, count what you are actually processing. Telecined and framerate-converted footage carries frames that are pixel-for-pixel copies of their predecessor. I took a 10-second 24 fps clip (240 frames), converted it to 59.94 fps with ffmpeg, and decoded both back to grayscale at 640×360 to compare every consecutive pair:

SourceFramesExact-duplicate pairsShare
original 24 fps24000.0%

Sixty percent. On this file, three of every five frames carry no information at all. That is not a quirk of the test pattern — it is arithmetic. Going 24 → 59.94 fps means 2.5 output frames per input frame, so at best 60% of the output is repetition.

Detecting them on a re-encoded file

Real files are not lossless, so "identical" needs a threshold. Pairs that should be identical still differ slightly after a lossy re-encode. Measuring mean absolute pixel difference per frame on an H.264 CRF 16 version of the same clip: p1 = 0.001, p5 = 0.002, p20 = 1.702, median = 3.362. What deduplication actually buys

Two things, and one thing it does not.

Compute: 20%. Upscaling 300 frames at 640×360 → 1280×720 with bicubic took 1865 ms on the full path and 1491 ms when reusing duplicate output frames — 6.22 ms per frame, 20.1% saved. If your upscale is a diffusion pass taking two seconds a frame, that same 20% is the difference between a 10-minute job and an 8-minute one.

Temporal filters: nothing. I tried to exploit duplicates for denoising by averaging each duplicate pair before upscaling. High-frequency energy ratio after averaging: 1.000. Zero noise reduction, and the reason is obvious in hindsight — averaging two identical frames returns the same frame. Duplicates give you no independent noise samples. If you run a multi-frame denoise, the effective temporal window on a telecined file is only 40% of the nominal frame count, and your settings are quietly four times weaker than you think.

Bitrate: no. This is the part that contradicts the obvious assumption. Encoding at CRF 20, keeping 59.94 fps produced 1078 KB for 600 frames; normalising back to 24 fps produced 1101 KB for 240 frames — the smaller file was marginally larger (ratio 1.021). x264 already spends almost nothing on a skip frame. Removing duplicates before encoding does not shrink the file; it only saves the work of producing them.

So the honest ordering is: deinterlace and denoise first (they must see every frame — a duplicate of a noisy frame is a different noisy frame, and dup detection after denoise will miss pairs that were identical before it), then deduplicate purely as a compute optimisation, then upscale. The output frames copied from a neighbour measured 77.92 dB PSNR against the full path with a maximum per-pixel deviation of 17 of 255 — under the noise floor of any H.264 delivery encode.

Is the file even interlaced? Here is a detector with a number

Everything above says "check a motion frame at 100%". That is a eyeball test with no threshold, and it fails in the one case that matters: a soft tape where the comb teeth are only a few levels deep. So I measured one.

The statistic is the asymmetry in adjacent-row steps. In a woven frame, even→odd transitions are between fields and odd→even transitions are within a field, so the two populations have different means. In a progressive frame they are the same population. comb = |mean(even→odd) − mean(odd→even)| / (mean(even→odd) + mean(odd→even)).

Contentcomb ratio
interlaced capture, 1 px/frame pan0.4906
same scene, progressive0.0000
interlaced capture, no motion at all0.0001

Two things to take from that table. The gap between 0.49 and 0.00 is wide enough that you do not need a threshold, just "not zero". And a static shot of interlaced footage scores 0.0001 — because with no motion the two fields are the same image, so there is nothing to detect. The detector needs motion, which is exactly why the advice says "a motion frame" and exactly why sampling a locked-off shot gives a false negative.

What deinterlacing actually costs, measured per row

A 50i frame contains two fields half a frame apart. One of them has to be invented. A single PSNR over the whole frame hides this, so I split it: even rows keep the captured field, odd rows are the interpolator's invention. Report the two separately and the filter ranking changes completely.

yadif is the worst of the four at every frequency from 0.04 to 0.24. The gap to the best filter runs 0.73 dB at the coarse end, crosses 3 dB at 0.10 cyc/px, peaks at 7.71 dB at 0.22, and then collapses to 6.45 dB at 0.25 because everything collapses there. Measured another way, on the same frame: at 0.12 cyc/px yadif's invented rows keep 72.9% of the sinusoid's amplitude, estdif 89.7%, bwdif 96.9%, w3fdif 97.1%. yadif is not a light deinterlacer, it is a spatial-only interpolator with a temporal nudge, and on texture it behaves like one.

yadif is also the default in so many recipes. That is the finding: the filter nearly everyone reaches for first is the one that costs the most detail, by 7 dB, in the band that matters for 480p tape.

The penalty can go negative, and that is not a bug. For w3fdif the even-row PSNR minus odd-row PSNR runs −0.26 dB at 0.16 cyc/px — the invented rows sit closer to the reference than the captured ones. The reason is temporal, not spatial: in a 50i frame neither field is at the reference instant. Both are 0.25 frame away. A motion-compensating interpolator can pull the invented row toward the midpoint while the captured row stays stale. So "even rows are ground truth" is a convenient fiction, and any quality metric that assumes it will mis-rank the motion-compensating filters.

The paradox: the filter that wins PSNR is the one you must not use

Read the PSNR table again. weave — no deinterlacing at all — wins every row, including a 0.01 dB even/odd penalty at 0.25 cyc/px. That is not a rounding artefact and it is not a harness bug. It is a property of comparing one timestamp: weave's odd rows are real pixels, merely stale by 0.5 frame, and a small phase error costs less in MSE than a blurred interpolation does. Measured at 0.12 cyc/px: weave keeps 99.9% of the amplitude with a +10.8° phase error, versus yadif's 72.9% amplitude with −10.9°. Amplitude loss is what PSNR punishes; a phase shift it barely notices.

The artefact that a phase shift produces has a name, and it is not in the PSNR. It is comb. Measuring both on the same output:

All four real filters stay within 2.5 dB of each other for the same range, and their spread shrinks as motion increases (9.1 dB at 0.25 px/frame down to 2.0 dB at 8 px/frame): filter choice only matters on slow content, which is the opposite of the intuition that fast motion needs the fancier algorithm. interpolation error depends on content, weave's error depends on speed. That is the actual distinction, and it is why "weave is fine if it looks fine" survives casual inspection and fails on a panning shot.

Does deinterlacing before the upscale beat after? Yes, by less than a dB

The layer list says deinterlace first and non-negotiable. It never said how much it was worth. Measured on a 2D scene with sloped edges and texture, both pipelines against an analytically rendered 1440×1080 ground truth (not a nearest-neighbour repeat, which flatters whichever pipeline is blurrier):

Half a decibel, consistent across all four filters and both directions of the ± sign, so it is real rather than noise. Half a decibel is not nothing, but it is not the 7 dB that choosing the right interpolator is worth. Order matters less than filter choice, which is the opposite of where all the advice puts the emphasis. Do both — deinterlace first and pick something better than yadif — but if you can only fix one, fix the filter.

What it costs to deinterlace a file that was never interlaced

Also asserted above with no number: do not deinterlace progressive content. Measured on progressive 2D content at three pan speeds:

yadif and bwdif are inert on progressive input — 59.7 dB is transparency, they detect no comb and pass the frame through. estdif and w3fdif are not: 55.9 and 54.1 dB means they rewrite rows even when nothing asked them to, and w3fdif's 5.7 dB is visible on a gradient. So the folk rule is wrong in both directions. Deinterlacing progressive content is free with yadif and bwdif, and a real loss with the two filters that are otherwise better interpolators. Marking a progressive file as interlaced and running w3fdif over it is the one combination that hurts.

One case where the question is unanswerable. Feed a progressive frame containing pure vertical detail at 0.25 cyc/px — a hard weave pattern, a striped shirt, a venetian blind — and bwdif drops it from 56 dB to 21.16 dB. That is not a bug: an alternating-every-2-rows pattern is comb, and no detector can distinguish the two from a single frame. The practical consequence: vertical stripes at half-Nyquist get destroyed by every deinterlacer, so check a striped surface before trusting an automatic decision. It also explains why a per-file comb ratio of 0.49 is trustworthy but a per-file decision on a striped shirt is not.

Reproducing the deinterlace measurements

import subprocess, numpy as np

W, H, N, PAN = 720, 540, 8, 1.0
def vert(f, dy):                     # analytic scene, any sub-pixel offset
    y = np.arange(H)[:, None] + dy
    return np.repeat(0.5 + 0.35*np.sin(2*np.pi*f*y), W, axis=1).astype(np.float32)

# 50i: even rows from field at t, odd rows from field at t + 0.5 frame
vf = np.empty((N, H, W), np.float32)
for k in range(N):
    a, b = vert(0.12, PAN*k), vert(0.12, PAN*k + 0.5*PAN)
    fr = np.empty((H, W), np.float32); fr[0::2] = a[0::2]; fr[1::2] = b[1::2]
    vf[k] = fr

# reference is the UNSEEN progressive frame at the temporal midpoint -- never a
# transformed copy of the input, or the test compares a frame with itself
gt = np.stack([vert(0.12, PAN*k + 0.25*PAN) for k in range(N)])

def run(v, vf_filter):
    raw = (np.clip(v, 0, 1)*255 + 0.5).astype(np.uint8).tobytes()
    out = subprocess.run(['ffmpeg','-v','error','-f','rawvideo','-pix_fmt','gray',
                          '-s',f'{W}x{H}','-r','25','-i','-','-vf',vf_filter,
                          '-f','rawvideo','-pix_fmt','gray','-'],
                         input=raw, capture_output=True).stdout
    return np.frombuffer(out, np.uint8).reshape(-1, H, W).astype(np.float32)/255

def psnr(a, b):
    return 10*np.log10(1.0/((a-b)**2).mean())

for name, f in [('yadif','yadif=mode=send_frame:deint=all'),
                ('bwdif','bwdif=mode=send_frame:deint=all'),
                ('w3fdif','w3fdif=filter=complex:deint=all')]:
    o = run(vf, f)
    o = o[::len(o)//N][-N:]                      # send_field emits 2 frames per input
    print(name, 'odd rows',
          round(psnr(o[:,1::2], gt[:,1::2]), 2),
          'even rows', round(psnr(o[:,0::2], gt[:,0::2]), 2))

# comb ratio -- the metric PSNR cannot see
def comb(img):
    d = np.abs(np.diff(img, axis=0)).mean(axis=1)
    return abs(d[0::2].mean() - d[1::2].mean()) / (d[0::2].mean() + d[1::2].mean())

Four mistakes this measured section exists to prevent

Every number above is from a harness that was wrong at least once first. The failures are worth more than the results, because each one produced a plausible number.

  1. Measured phase on the mean of moving frames. Averaging a panning sinusoid over a full period cancels it to DC, so every filter appeared to keep ~99% of its amplitude and weave appeared to have 0.0° of phase error — a perfect score for the option that leaves visible comb. Fixed by measuring one frame against the truth for that same timestamp, and by measuring odd rows separately: even and odd rows carry symmetric errors (+0.25 and −0.25 frame) that cancel to zero in a row-mean.
  2. Scaled the vertical axis but not the horizontal one when rendering the 2× ground truth. Every filter then scored an identical 9.21 dB. When four different filters agree to two decimals, the reference is misaligned, not the filters equivalent. Fixed by sharing one scene→pixel pitch across both axes, which moved the result to 32.6 vs 32.0 dB.
  3. Used a sloped-edge statistic that read 3.3 for every filter, including weave. It was measuring the scene's own edge slope, not tearing — the intercept dominated the metric. Fixed by switching to the adjacent-row asymmetry, which reads 0.4793 for weave and 0.0012 for a real deinterlacer, a 400× separation instead of 4%.
  4. Assumed even rows were ground truth. In a 50i frame neither field is at the reference instant; both are 0.25 frame away. That is why w3fdif can score a negative penalty — its invented rows are better aligned in time than the captured ones. Any metric built on "even rows are truth" mis-ranks the motion-compensating filters, which is exactly the class you most want to rank correctly.

Three of those four produced numbers in a believable range. Only the 9.21 dB tie announced itself as impossible. The asserts that caught the rest were the cheap ones: a static input must come back bit-identical, white noise must pass at ratio 1.00, and two filters must not be allowed to score the same to two decimals on different algorithms.

The honest limit

Restoration recovers structure, not truth. A face at 480p contains enough information to infer that a face is there and roughly what it looked like. It does not contain the eyelashes. A model that draws convincing eyelashes has invented them, and on a family photograph that is a strange thing to do without noticing.

The test that matters: does the restored version look like the person you remember, or like a plausible stranger? If you cannot tell, compare against a still from the same era at higher resolution. The model's idea of a face and your grandmother's actual face are different things, and only one of them is in the file.
